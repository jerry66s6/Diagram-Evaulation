Classifying good and bad diagrams from human scores

Diagram Evaluation · November 5, 2026 · branch classification

The 13 human scores separate good diagrams from bad ones well. On 100 held-out test diagrams, logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and an SVM reach AUC 0.964 to 0.976 and accuracy 0.90 to 0.92. All four beat simply averaging the scores (AUC 0.936, accuracy 0.85). They cannot be told apart from each other, so logistic regression is the natural choice: it is the simplest, and its weights can be read directly.

Setup

Input
The 13 updated human scores for each diagram, 1 to 10: 5 Correctness and 8 Beauty. No images and no judge scores.
Label
150 positive (good) and 150 negative (deliberately made bad with inserted errors, from 23 error types). The label does not come from the human scores.
Test
HS201 to HS300, scored once at the end: 53 bad and 47 good.
Train/val
5-fold on HS001 to HS200 (160/40 in each fold), balanced on label and difficulty. Each method's settings and its good/bad cut are chosen on val, then it is refit on all 200.
Methods
Decision tree, logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and an RBF SVM. Two rules with no training are the comparison: the mean and the lowest of the 13 scores.

  1. Combining the scores beats averaging them. The four methods are 0.028 to 0.040 above the mean of the 13 on test AUC, and each difference's interval excludes zero.
  2. Those four cannot be separated. Gradient boosting has the best val AUC (0.961, SVM 0.960), and logistic regression the best test AUC (0.976, gradient boosting 0.975). Their test intervals overlap heavily.
  3. A single decision tree is no better than the plain mean (test AUC 0.934 vs 0.936), though its rules are the easiest to read.
  4. The test scores may run a little high. Most methods score above their val AUC on test, including the plain mean (0.917 to 0.936), although the test set has fewer easy diagrams. Val AUC of 0.95 to 0.96 is the more cautious figure.

Style uniformity, presence, and details matter most. Logistic regression agrees: its largest standardized weights are style uniformity (−0.83), presence (−0.65), and details (−0.64). Humans tended to lower several dimensions together, so these importances share credit among correlated scores.

Style uniformity mostly drops where style really changed. Its human mean is 9.37 on positives, 6.13 on negatives with a Beauty error, 5.05 on negatives with an enlarged or shrunk label, and 8.13 on the other negatives. The results do not rest on it alone: SVM val AUC moves from 0.960 to 0.946 without it.

Limits

  • Human scores must exist. A new diagram has none, so these models cannot score it as they are. Repeating this on judge scores is a separate experiment: it needs the judge's Beauty scores for HS146 to HS300 first, and HS201 to HS300 suits it as a test set because the correctness prompt was not tuned on them.
  • Only these error types were tested. Diagrams that are bad in other ways, such as new problems in LLM-generated figures, have not been tried.
  • The test set has 100 diagrams. One diagram moves accuracy by 1 point, so small gaps between methods mean little.
  • The test set is not entirely fresh. These diagrams were also in earlier exploratory runs of the same methods. The methods and setting grids did not change after those runs.

As a check on the pipeline, shuffling the labels drops test AUC to about 0.5.
